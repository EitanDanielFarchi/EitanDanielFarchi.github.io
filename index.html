My research asks one question: how do we establish confidence in software whose behavior is too large, too nondeterministic, or too opaque to inspect directly?

The method has stayed the same from concurrent programs to large language models. Build an explicit model of intended behavior, derive a coverage or validity criterion from it, generate tests that stress its boundaries, and then validate the measuring instrument itself. I apply the same habit to my own papers: they are posted openly, tested by readers, and corrected in public.

How I publish

A home page instead of a gatekeeper

I follow the model Larry Wasserman set out in A World Without Referees: research is disseminated openly, readers judge it for themselves, and quality control happens through public critique rather than private approval. This page is where that happens for my work.

Preprint first

Every paper goes on arXiv as soon as it is written, before and regardless of any venue decision. When a new paper is posted, I announce it here and to colleagues working on the same problem.

Read it, then judge it

Each paper below links to the full text. Venue names are listed for reference, and you can hide them. How often a paper has been used and cited tells you more than where it appeared.

Critique is invited

Every paper has a Critique this paper button. Send comments, objections, counter-examples, or a full review by email. I answer each one, and with your permission I post the exchange in the ledger below.

Errors are fixed in the open

When a reader finds a mistake, I thank them by name, post a revised version, and record the change in the errata log. Earlier versions stay available on arXiv.

Reviewing is scholarship

I write public reviews of other people's papers and list them here. Reviews of my own work are listed alongside, including the critical ones.

“If you rely on referees to filter papers for you then I think you are making a huge error.” Larry Wasserman, A World Without Referees (2012)

    About

    Biography

    I have been at IBM Research – Haifa since 1992, and was appointed Distinguished Engineer in 2016 for leading the transformation of IBM's quality-assurance practices. My work combines research in software engineering and mathematics with hands-on experience across the whole development cycle.

    I led the software testing project cluster, conceiving tools and methods for testing, analysis, and review and deploying them across many projects. A central contribution is Combinatorial Test Design, which uses mathematical optimization to cover a set of requirements and their interactions with far fewer tests, typically less than half. It underpins an IBM test-outsourcing and platform business. I also drove the shift-left strategy in firmware, created IBM's review-moderator certification program, and consulted for Western Digital from 2014 to 2022.

    For the last several years my work has focused on testing and validating systems built with machine learning and, more recently, generative AI.
